Agent Governance Card

State governments are moving past chatbots into agentic AI: software that acts. Agents can use tools, touch data, receive and send files, and complete multi-step tasks with autonomy. Yet the IT governance machinery states rely on today, including intake, security review, procurement, and audit, wasn't built for software that acts. The introduction of agents in government presents governance challenges.

A first central step to address them is to ensure visibility and transparency on what agents do in public organisations. What is an agent allowed to do? What did it touch? Who answers for it? How does a person step in? And what does it cost to leave? These are questions that cannot and should not be avoided.

The Agent Governance Card was developed by The Agentic State and Center for Civic Futures to help teams address these questions. It exists to assess and record information, bringing structured, comparable transparency to agentic deployments that interact directly with the public or materially influence a decision affecting the public or state resources.

Who uses it

Most often a product team, the people who build or configure the agent. A CIO's office, a procurement officer, or an auditor may also fill one in. When a vendor is engaged, the accountable public agency fills it.

When

Most often before an agent gets production access to state data, tools, or users. Also earlier, as a checklist during development, or later, for systems already live. Once filled, the card is revisited on a rolling basis.

What it delivers

A snapshot of one deployment: capabilities, data used, who answers for it, how a person steps in, and more. From the answers, the card computes an Impact Level that routes the deployment to proportionate obligations.

The backbone of the card is its schema. It builds on the governance frameworks that matter most for public deployments, including OMB's memoranda on AI use and procurement, the UK's Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard and NIST's AI Risk Management Framework, and on frontier research on agents: identity and delegation, autonomy and reversibility, the cost of leaving a vendor.

The schema is composed of twelve dimensions, spanning what an agent does and touches, who answers for it, its risks, and more. Across them, 47 fields: for each, the plain-language question a team answers, the values an answer can take, and the rationale behind the field. Fourteen fields, plus the Impact Level, form the public layer, marked with a triangle (▲), the set of information that would be ideally made public.

The schema can be filled in two ways. Teams can work through it by hand, or fill it interactively by running the skill, which acts as the interviewer: it asks the questions one at a time, helps with the hard ones, computes the Impact Level, and emits the filled card. The skill installs in any assistant that supports the open Agent Skills standard, and can be opened directly in the repository of the agent being built, where it reads the answers from the specs and code already there.

Governments are moving past chatbots into agentic AI: software that combines the intelligence of LLMs with the ability to act in real systems. Agents can use tools, touch data, send messages, file records, and complete multi-step tasks autonomously. This opens the door to more efficient, effective, and tailored public services. It also raises a governance problem. When agents perform functions traditionally performed by public servants, they carry none of the legal accountability attached to a person. The intake, security review, procurement, and audit machinery states lean on today wasn't built for software that acts. The first requirement to address this is transparency: visibility, inside and outside government, on what agents are doing.

The Agent Governance Card serves this purpose by guiding government teams deploying agents to surface the information that governance depends on. It is structured around a schema of twelve dimensions, spanning the agent's connection to citizen data through to its oversight structure and override paths.

The Card brings structured, comparable transparency to agentic deployments that interact directly with the public or materially influence a decision affecting the public or state resources. It is to be used most prominently before deployment, as the record a team completes before an agent gets production access to state data, tools, or users. It also works earlier, during design and development, helping teams settle a governance approach as they build, and later, for live deployments whose details are not yet visible or are recorded without structure. Where it sits in a state's own process is the state's call: some will attach it to procurement, some to the pre-production review, some to both. Structure of the Card

The Card borrows its record shape from four transparency instruments: the UK's Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard, Canada's Algorithmic Impact Assessment, the EU AI Act, and the two US federal memos on AI use and AI procurement (OMB M-25-21 and M-25-22). It takes its agent-specific concepts, such as agent identity, delegation, automation boundaries, and oversight cadence, from NIST, the Cloud Security Alliance, the A2A and MCP protocol specifications, and recent academic work on agent governance.

The two federal memos are the primary reference models, so a state's Cards roll up against the federal vocabulary. The rationale of each field in the schema names the precedent behind it.

The Card has twelve dimensions. The first seven (Part 1) apply to every agent, and six of those answers compute its Impact Level. If the level is not Low, the remaining five dimensions apply (Part 2), asking for more detail on the risk surface of the deployment.

In total, the Card has forty-seven fields. Nine of them take a fixed list of values and, together with the Impact Level, make Cards comparable across states: agent type, capability, high-impact determination, data sensitivity, autonomy level, reversibility, consequence severity, government function, and lifecycle stage.

Fourteen fields are marked with a triangle (▲). These are the fields that, with the Impact Level, form the information that would at minimum ideally be made public: name, what it is, purpose, agent type, location, autonomy level, accountable owner, deploying agency and contact point, government function, lifecycle stage, public summary published, disclosure, appeal route, and who is affected.

Impact Level

The level is computed deterministically from six answers, so that the same answers always give the same level:

  1. OMB M-25-21 high-impact determination. If the agent's output is a principal basis for a decision with legal, material, or significant effect on rights, benefits, health and safety, or critical infrastructure, the Card is High. A human signing off does not lower this: the memo is explicit that a use can be high-impact with or without human oversight.
  2. Operating risk, based on five answers about how the agent operates: what data it touches, how much it does before a human is involved, how hard its actions are to undo, how bad the worst plausible outcome is, and whether it combines untrusted input, protected data, and the power to act with no human in the loop (the safety red-line). If any of these five answers is unknown, the level cannot be Low until it is resolved.

The final Impact Level is the higher of the two results and can be Low, Moderate, or High. Low Impact deployments are invited to fill Part 1 only. Moderate Impact deployments are also invited to complete Part 2. When the level is Moderate only because an answer is unknown, Part 2 narrows to two dimensions, automation and approval detail and oversight and monitoring. High Impact deployments are invited to complete both parts but also follow the OMB M-25-21 minimum practices for high-impact AI: pre-deployment testing, a documented impact assessment, independent review, ongoing monitoring with periodic human review, operator training, a tested fail-safe, an appeal route, a public feedback channel, and named senior sign-off.

What to do with a filled-in Card

A filled Card belongs, at minimum, in the state's internal register of agents. We recommend also publishing the public layer, the fourteen ▲ fields plus the Impact Level, so the public can see what agents their government is running. As more states publish, the comparative picture across states that no one has today begins to form.

Frequently asked questions

1. What data should I use in filling the Card?

The card is designed to record what is true at go-live, so it should be filled from production materials rather than from the team's description of the system wherever possible. That key evidence comes from three sources:

  • The code and configuration of the agent itself: tool and MCP server definitions, OAuth scopes and service-account permissions, network egress rules, system prompts, UI disclosure text, feature flags and kill switches, logging and alerting config, and the eval and red-team suite. These settle the fields on tools reached, the safety red-line, automation boundary, logging, monitoring, and disclosure as facts.
  • Documents the state or deploying body already holds, possibly across several offices, such as: a data classification policy and the classification of each dataset touched; a system security plan or authorization package, ideally listing data flows and interconnections; a privacy impact assessment; a contract, statement of work, and data-sharing agreements, which carry vendor, model rights, portability, and exit terms; an AI use case inventory entry; an incident response plan and business continuity impact analysis; a governance body's charter; and the business case. These feed data sensitivity, the high-impact determination, accountability, procurement, oversight cadence, and consequence severity.
  • Public information, such as the statute(s) and rules for the program served, which almost always already define the appeals route(s) the card should point to; AI model and system cards, terms of use, and SOC 2 or FedRAMP status; the state's published AI policy; service usage statistics and the agency's language access plan, for who is affected.

Using the skill. When filling the card using the skill through a coding agent, connect the agent's own repository first (including infrastructure-as-code and deployment config, not just application code). Add an evidence folder with the documents above, or read-only access to where they live: the IAM directory, the CMDB or application portfolio, the contract register, and the AI inventory.

2. Who should deploy the Card?

The team. The core user is the team that builds, procures, or operates the agent: the product owner, the engineers or the integrator's staff, and the official who will be named as accountable owner (6.1). For them, the Card is a structured way to see their own deployment as a whole. A team filling in the card should bear in mind that gaps are data: "unknown" and "not in place" are complete answers, so a team can fill the Card honestly at the design stage and refill it as the gaps close.

The state. At state level the Card becomes a systematic practice rather than one team's exercise, potentially overseen and mandated by a state CIO or other relevant body. In a scenario of systematic adoption, the Card might slot into existing security review, data classification, privacy assessment, and procurement processes. A state might fork the Card to fit into its governance process (e.g. by adding fields, tightening value lists, mapping its own data classification tiers to the card).

The nation, the ecosystem. If multiple states publish the public layer of their Cards, the result is a comparable picture of the agents governments are running, across states, in one vocabulary. Residents can see what acts on their behalf. Auditors, ombudsmen, and civil society groups can ask a state what it based a decision on and be pointed at a record. Teams in one state can find deployments like theirs in another, which is the pooled inventory of evaluated cases that state AI leads have asked for and that no organisation currently maintains. Vendors face the same questions in every state, which is a smaller burden than fifteen different questionnaires.

Ecosystem impact is greater if the schema is maintained. AI changes fast enough that keeping a review current is a standing cost; centralising that cost is much of the point. The Center for Civic Futures, as the community of practice for state AI officers, is the natural steward for US states, and the Card is written so that another body could take the role. The commitment is modest at this stage: the Card is offered as a common base that states adopt and amend, the amendments flow back into the next version, and whether it becomes a formal standard is a question the community answers by using it.

3. Who should fill in the Card: agency or vendor?

The deploying agency is responsible for filling in the Card, as public accountability rests with the public body. While vendors may provide technical details or contribute to drafts where systems are integrator-built or operated on the state's behalf, the agency must verify and own the final Card.

4. Does using the skill expose any internal data?

No. The skill operates locally or within your controlled environment to assist in gathering and structuring the card. No information leaves your environment in the process of executing the skill. The person filling out the card retains full control and responsibility for deciding which parts of the completed record are shared internally or published publicly.

The card's schema. Twelve dimensions. For each field: the plain-language question a team answers, the values an answer can take, and the rationale behind it. Every field takes one of four answer statuses: answered, unknown, not applicable, not in place. A missing mechanism recorded plainly is a governance finding, not a failure. Before filling · scope and unit of analysis

▶§Is a card required, and for what?

A card is required if the system interacts directly with the public, or materially influences a decision-making process with effect on the public or state resources, and takes actions or produces outputs beyond returning static search results.

Out of scope: embedded commodity features (autocomplete, spell-check, in-app summarization) and purely predictive back-office models. Record those in the AI inventory; use an impact-assessment instrument for consequential predictive models. For the commodity carve-out, apply OMB M-25-22 §2(b)'s two-part test: exempt only if the product is widely available for commercial use and the AI is embedded in a product with substantial non-AI purposes. A product used primarily for its AI is in scope.

One card = one agent deployment in one operational context. Where a platform serves many units or agencies: one parent card for the shared layer (model, hosting and platform, vendor, identity architecture, exit terms) plus one short deployment addendum per context (purpose, data touched, autonomy, function).

RationaleThe sensitivity class in 3.1 names a ceiling; this field names the datasets under it. The UK standard records operational data on its own sheet. Research on governing agents (Nannini et al.) treats the inventory of what an agent can read and write as the starting point for every other control. A reviewer needs the names to check the classification, and a resident needs them to know where the agent's answers come from.

3.3
Tools, systems & APIs it can reachWhich tools, systems, and APIs can it reach — everything it can call or act on?
list

RationaleThe list of everything the agent can call or act on. The Cloud Security Alliance's identity framework requires agents to declare their capabilities and keep to a defined perimeter. The MCP specification describes agents' access as a set of tools and resources. The delegation literature calls this the agent's resource scope (South et al.). For software that acts, this list is the boundary of what can go wrong. No register written for earlier AI systems asks it, because a classifier or a chatbot has no tools to list.

▶4Autonomy and human control5 fields · 1 ▲
4.1
Autonomy level ▲How much does the agent do before a human is involved? ("Acts" = any effect outside the conversation.)
one valuecomparablecomputes the level
informsrecommendsacts_with_approvalacts_without_approval

RationaleThe highest level the agent reaches for any action it performs. The four levels are the card's own. They are inspired by the treatment of human oversight in US frameworks (NIST AI RMF, OMB M-25-21) and by the Cloud Security Alliance's autonomy tiers. “Informs” gives a user information but does not suggest what to decide or do, e.g. a case summary, an answer to "what documents do I need." “Recommends” proposes a decision, action, or ranking for a person to take: a draft eligibility determination, a "likely fraud" flag, a prioritized queue. The test is whether the output tells a user which way to go; if it does, the agent recommends, even when phrased as a draft. “Acts with approval” prepares an action that takes effect outside the conversation once a person confirms it: a pre-filled email sent on click. “Acts without approval” takes effect on its own: sends, files, updates records, pays.

4.2
Reversibility of its actionsOnce the agent's output has taken effect, is there a way to undo it?
one valuecomparablecomputes the level
fully_reversiblereversible_with_efforthard_to_reverse

RationaleCanada's Algorithmic Impact Assessment grades decisions by whether they can be reversed. The card asks the same question and fixes the moment to judge it: when the output reaches a person or a system that may act on it. What is judged is the effect on that person, not the artefact. A payment can be clawed back and a notice corrected, but the resident may already have spent the money or acted on the notice. "Fully reversible": nothing has left the system yet, or a built-in undo restores the previous state before anyone relies on it (a draft a person edits). "Reversible with effort": the output has reached someone and a separate corrective action can still undo the effect in time (a follow-up email, a corrected notice). "Hard to reverse": the effect cannot be fully undone by the state alone, whether or not the artefact can be recalled (a payment made, a decision communicated, a public post seen, a record deleted).

4.3
Maximum consequence severityIf the output is wrong, what is in reach? Consider: how many people or records it can touch, whether it moves or decides money, and whether it can cause physical harm or the loss of a right.
one valuecomparablecomputes the level
negligiblelimitedserioussevere

RationaleThe question asks for three facts that can be checked, and the value is read from them. The levels are anchored to concrete markers. “Negligible”: output stays inside the team, touches few people, no money and no rights involved. “Limited”: anything published outside the team or with a financial effect. “Serious”: wrong guidance that people can act on, or more than 10,000 people or records in reach. “Severe”: physical harm, the loss of a right, or an irreversible effect at scale. Concerns about what a model might do if it escapes its controls belong to the safety red-line (4.4) and the agentic hazards (5.2).

4.4
Safety red-line (the agentic trifecta)Without a human in the loop, does it combine all three of: acting on untrusted or external input; access to non-public personal or protected data; and the ability to change anything outside the conversation? (All three at once, with no human in the loop, is the red-line.)
one valuecomputes the level
noyes_with_compensating_controlsyes_unresolved

RationaleThree properties together make an agent dangerous without a person in the loop: it takes input it cannot trust (from the public or from an external system), it can read non-public personal or protected data, and it can change something outside the conversation. An agent with all three can be manipulated through its input into leaking or acting on protected data, with nothing to stop it. Based on the Spanish data-protection authority (AEPD) and Meta's "rule of 2" (via Nannini et al.). Three answers are possible. No. Yes, with compensating controls: all three are present, and the team names the controls that contain the risk; the level is at least Moderate. Yes, unresolved: all three are present and nothing contains them; the level is High, and the card recommends not entering production until one of the three is removed or controls are in place.

4.5
How a person stops or overrides itHow does a person stop or override it?
free text

RationaleWork on autonomous systems commonly includes a way to stop them. Levels of Autonomy describes an off-switch at its top tier, Su et al. discuss safety interlocks, and the A2A protocol has task states in which work can wait or be cancelled. At High impact, the federal memo (OMB M-25-21, §4(b)) requires a fail-safe; the card asks for one that has been tested, not only described.

list

RationaleThis list draws on a survey of autonomy-induced attack surfaces (Su et al.) and on literature on multi-agent failure modes. 5.3

RationaleThe federal memo (OMB M-25-21, §4(b)) requires pre-deployment testing for high-impact AI, and Canada's Algorithmic Impact Assessment asks whether the system was tested. A completed impact assessment, a vendor's benchmark, or a checked box in a register is not an evaluation of this system. If none was done, the answer is No.

RationaleTransparency regimes ask whether a public description exists: the UK standard's public tier, Canada's publication of assessment results on its open government portal, the EU's public database. The card asks whether a plain-language summary of this agent is published, and where.

An agent used only by staff, with no contact with the public, answers "not applicable".

high if any: red-line yes_unresolved; acts without approval and (hard to reverse or severity ≥ serious); sensitive data and acts without approval.

moderate if any, and not High: personal or sensitive data; acts with or without approval; severity ≥ limited and not informs-only; red-line yes_with_compensating_controls.

low otherwise. Any unknown among the five floors the tier at Moderate until resolved.

Final levelthe higher of ① and ②

Recommendations to resolve, never prohibitions: complete a pending determination; break one leg of an unresolved red-line or name compensating controls; resolve an unknown input. A Low result for a public-facing agent is confirmed by a named reviewer outside the build team.

What each level requires.

  • Low → Part 1 only (public-facing: plus reviewer confirmation).
  • Moderate → Part 2. All five dimensions for a substantive Moderate; dimensions 9 and 10 only when Moderate solely because of an unknown.
  • High → all of Part 2, plus the OMB M-25-21 §4(b) minimum practices: pre-deployment testing, a documented impact assessment, independent review, ongoing monitoring with periodic human review, operator training, a tested fail-safe, an appeal route, a public feedback channel, and named senior sign-off.

Part 2 · Moderate and High · 18 fields

▶8Identity, credentials, and delegation5 fields
8.1
Acting identityWhen it acts — sends an email, calls a system — whose identity does it use?
free text

RationaleAn agent acts under some identity, and that identity impacts on who is accountable for the action. If it uses a staff member's login, the log shows that person acting; if it uses a shared account, the log shows no one. The Cloud Security Alliance's identity framework and the authenticated-delegation research (South et al.) favour giving an agent an identity of its own, and NIST's concept paper on agent identity explores the same question.

8.2
Human principalOn whose authority does it act — who is the human principal or sponsor?
free text

RationaleWork on agent identity ties the agent to a person: the Cloud Security Alliance requires a named sponsor and a chain of sponsoring principals, the delegation research (South et al.) ties the agent's credential to the user's identity, and NIST's concept paper on agent identity discusses linking agents to user identities. This field records who that person or role is. It differs from the accountable owner (6.1): the owner answers for the system, the principal is the person on whose behalf it acts.

8.3
Authorized scopeWhat is it allowed to do: (a) which tasks and workflows; (b) which tools, data, and APIs?
free text

RationaleThe delegation research separates what an agent is allowed to do (task scope) from what it is allowed to reach (resource scope). Written down together, the two describe the agent's authorization. The card asks for the description; the technical means of enforcing it are still being standardized, and this field does not require them.

8.4
Sub-agentsCan it create other agents? If so, can they do more than it can (they shouldn't), and is it recorded which agent created which?
yes / no + detail

RationaleThe Cloud Security Alliance has a rule of constrained delegation: no agent may give a sub-agent more privilege than it holds itself, and the creator of each agent is recorded. This field checks both, keeping the question practical, not hypothetical.

8.5
Traceability to a personCan its actions be traced back to the responsible human? How?
yes / no + detail

RationaleNIST's concept paper on agent identity asks how an agent's actions can be tied to a responsible person. The research on agent visibility proposes agent identifiers for a related purpose. This field asks for the mechanism that makes the trace possible, such as an identifier in the logs, not a statement that it is possible in principle. A Cloud Security Alliance survey reports that many organizations cannot yet clearly tell an agent's actions from a person's.

▶9Automation and approval2 fields
9.1
Automation boundaryFor each action or decision type: is it autonomous, does it confirm with a human first, or is it human-only?
table
modesautonomousconfirm_firsthuman_only

RationaleThe Part 1 autonomy answer (4.1) records the highest level the agent reaches. This field records the level for each type of action, so that "acts with approval" can be checked against a real list of what the agent does. The analysis of agents under EU law (Nannini et al.) suggest the boundary to be declared at this level of detail. For instance, a statement like "all financial actions require approval" is too coarse: it says nothing about the notification, the record update, or the flag on a case file that happen alongside the payment.

9.2
Actions requiring human approvalWhich actions always require human approval?
list

RationaleCanada's Algorithmic Impact Assessment requires human decision points at its higher impact levels; the Levels of Autonomy framework lets a user name the conditions under which an agent must stop; the A2A protocol has a state in which a task waits for further input before it continues (INPUT_REQUIRED). This field lists the actions that always require approval, whatever the context, as a summary of the "confirm_first" and "human_only" rows in 9.1.

▶10Oversight and monitoring5 fields
10.1
LogsWhat is logged? Is it enough to reconstruct a decision? Does the log distinguish what the agent did on its own from what a person told it to do?
free text

RationaleChan et al. specify the log categories (tool use, reasoning, memory); Nannini et al. add the agent-native requirement — the log must distinguish what the agent initiated from what a person directed.

10.2
Live monitoring and alertsIs there live monitoring, and what triggers an alert?
yes / no + detail

RationaleThe federal memo (OMB M-25-21, §4(b)) requires ongoing monitoring for high-impact AI. The research on agent visibility (Chan et al.) proposes real-time flagging of agent behavior, and the Cloud Security Alliance's framework proposes detecting when an agent's behavior departs from its declared intent. The card asks two things: whether monitoring exists, and what makes it raise an alert. A dashboard that no one is alerted by is not monitoring.

10.3
Oversight cadenceHow is the agent overseen: continuously, at set intervals, or only when something happens?
one value
event_triggeredperiodic_intervalcontinuous

RationaleResearch on oversight structures in the public sector (Schmitz et al.) finds that reviewing an agent only at fixed points, such as launch or an incident, does not work, because the agent's behavior changes between those points; oversight of an agent usually needs to be continuous. This field records which of the three the deployment has. Continuous: automated monitoring runs all the time, with alerts (10.2). Periodic: a person reviews the agent's activity at a set interval, weekly or monthly for example; the interval is stated. Event-triggered: review happens only after a complaint, an incident, or a change. Periodic review is a common arrangement in government today, and recorded in North Carolina’s inventory approach.

10.4
Independent reviewWho reviews the agent independently (not on the build team)?
free text

RationaleThe federal checklist requires review by an office not involved in development. Canada's Algorithmic Impact Assessment requires more peer review as impact rises. This field records who performs that review here. In many deployments no such reviewer exists yet, and "not in place" is the answer that records it.

10.5
Drift triggerWhat change(s) to the agent or its use would require this card to be re-done?
free text

RationaleNannini et al.’s analysis of agents under EU law suggests a stated threshold that separates normal adaptation from a substantial modification of the system. Without one, a record stays unchanged while the system changes underneath it. This field names the threshold for this agent, for example a new tool, a new data source, a new user group, or a change in autonomy level, so that both the team and a reviewer know when the card is out of date.

▶11Override, escalation, and redress3 fields · 2 ▲
11.1
Appeal route ▲How does someone affected by the agent’s output challenge it?
free text

RationaleAppeal fields converge across the federal memo (OMB M-25-21, §4(b)), the UK's Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard, Canada's Algorithmic Impact Assessment. This field records the route for this agent: whom a person contacts, and what happens next. Many deployments have none, and "not in place" is the answer that records it.

11.2
Affected people ▲Who is affected by it, including indirectly?
list

RationaleThis field requires the record to name everyone affected, not only the users, building on Nannini et al. The recipient of an email the agent drafted is affected without ever having seen the agent; so is a resident whose case it flagged. This field asks for a list of who, not for an audience size — differently from North Carolina's inventory approach.

11.3
Safeguard for fully-automated decisionsWhen the agent makes a decision with consequences for a person and no one reviews it, what additional safeguard applies?
free text

RationaleUK-ATRS carries this question as its GDPR Article 22 field. A person has the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing without a safeguard. This field records the safeguard that applies here, for example a right to request human review, a sampling audit of automated decisions, or an automatic hold on decisions above a threshold. If the agent never decides without review, the answer is "not applicable".

▶12Cost and lock-in3 fields
12.1
Vendor and contractWho is the supplier, on what procurement route, and under what data-access terms?
free text

RationaleUK-ATRS's third-party block is the model for this field — supplier name, procurement procedure, and data-access terms as a discrete record. The federal inventory records the vendor name. This field takes the information-richer approach.

12.2
Expected valueWhat is it compared against, and what is the expected benefit over the status quo?
free text

RationaleValue should be judged against the existing human process, not against zero (Schmitz et al.). This field records the comparison and the expected benefit. If the card is filled pre-deployment, this ensures that pilot results and, later, the renewal price can be measured against a stated baseline rather than a remembered one.

12.3
Exit termsIf the state had to leave this vendor, could it? Answer each item of the US-OMB M-25-22 checklist.
free text
checklistknowledge transferdata + model portability (prompts, traces, fine-tunes, vector stores)licensing/pricing transparencycode/model rightsvendor-change noticerollback & closeout

RationaleThe federal AI procurement memo (OMB M-25-22) lists what a contract must provide for an agency to be able to change vendors: transfer of knowledge, portability of data and models, transparency on licensing and pricing, rights to code and models produced under the contract, advance notice of changes on the vendor's side, and a plan for rollback and closeout. The card adds the agent-specific items under portability: prompts, interaction traces, fine-tuned models, and vector stores, which are the parts of an agent deployment that most often stay with the vendor. This field records, item by item, which of these the contract provides.

Publishing the card

Once a card is filled, our invitation to states is to make it visible. At minimum it belongs in an internal register. We recommend publishing it openly for citizens to see, in particular the public layer: the fourteen fields, plus the Impact Level, that the schema sets aside for publication, marked ▲. As more states publish, the comparative picture states currently lack begins to form.
